# Explain how you'd choose between synchronous choreography (each service reacts to events) and a central orchestrator for a 7-step checkout, considering debuggability and change velocity.

**Difficulty:** Deep · **Levels:** Architect/Principal, SDE-1/2, SDE-2/3 · [↑ Choreography vs Orchestration](<../../../../03_Big_Data_and_Streaming/Streaming_and_Messaging/Event_Driven_Architecture/Choreography_vs_Orchestration.md>)

## Answer

To explain the choice between synchronous choreography and a central orchestrator in an event-driven architecture for a 7-step checkout process, let's start by understanding what these terms mean and their implications on debuggability and change velocity.

Synchronous choreography implies that each service reacts to events generated by others in real-time. In contrast, a central orchestrator coordinates the flow of steps, ensuring they are completed in sequence. We'll analyze both approaches considering our specific use case.

First, let's break down the checkout process into its 7 steps: cart initialization, item validation, payment selection, payment processing, order confirmation, shipping details collection, and final order acknowledgment. Each step involves different services like inventory management, payment gateway, and shipping service.

#### Triage

The question is primarily asking us to evaluate which architecture will offer better debuggability and adaptability to changes in our system. We need to consider the complexity of maintaining stateless vs. stateful systems, failure modes, and how easy it would be to identify issues and roll back changes.

From First Principles

Synchronous Choreography:

Debuggability: Tracing the flow becomes complex since events can occur at different times or out of order.
Change Velocity: Adding new steps requires updating multiple services, which could lead to integration issues if not done carefully.

Central Orchestrator:

Debuggability: Easier to trace the flow as all steps are orchestrated in a linear sequence.
Change Velocity: Centralizing logic can make updates more straightforward but introduces single points of failure and potential scalability bottlenecks.
Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Define Requirements:

Debuggability is crucial for diagnosing issues quickly, especially during peak times.
Change velocity is important to minimize downtime and ensure the system remains functional as new features are introduced.

Step 2: Evaluate Debuggability:

Synchronous choreography requires careful logging and tracing mechanisms since events may be asynchronous and non-deterministic.
A central orchestrator simplifies debugging by providing a clear, linear flow of steps.

Step 3: Assess Change Velocity:

Synchronous choreography can lead to complex inter-service dependencies, making changes risky and time-consuming.
A central orchestrator reduces the complexity of updating multiple services but could become a bottleneck if not well-designed.

Step 4: Consider Failure Modes:

Both approaches have failure modes. In synchronous choreography, failures in one service can cascade and affect others.
Central orchestrators need to handle retries, timeouts, and failover mechanisms more carefully to maintain the integrity of the overall flow.

Step 5: Weigh Trade-offs:

Synchronous choreography is beneficial when services are highly independent and events can be handled asynchronously.
A central orchestrator is preferable for complex workflows where steps need to be executed in a specific order, ensuring data consistency.

#### Closing Summary

Given the nature of our 7-step checkout process, a central orchestrator would likely offer better debuggability and change velocity. This approach simplifies tracing issues and reduces the complexity associated with updating multiple services. However, we must ensure robustness in handling retries, timeouts, and failover scenarios to mitigate potential bottlenecks.

#### Common Mistakes
Overlooking the importance of proper logging and error handling in synchronous choreography.
Neglecting the risk of a central orchestrator becoming a single point of failure without adequate redundancy or failover mechanisms.
