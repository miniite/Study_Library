# Conway's law is fighting your topic design: teams keep creating chatty cross-team topics that mirror the org chart. How would you reshape team topologies and topic ownership so the event model reflects business domains not reporting lines?

**Difficulty:** Expert · **Levels:** Architect/Principal, SDE-2/3 · [↑ Schema Formats and Registry](<../../../../TOOLS/Apache_Kafka/Kafka_Core/Schema_Formats_and_Registry.md>)

> ⚠️ Practice answer, not verified. Some answers contain mistakes; when in doubt, follow the lesson.

## Answer

#### Overview

When designing a Kafka-based event-driven architecture, it's crucial to align the topic design with business domains rather than organizational hierarchies, as suggested by Conway’s law. This ensures that communication and collaboration are more effective and efficient within the team and across teams. By reshaping team topologies and topic ownership, we can create a robust, scalable, and maintainable system.

#### Triaging the Question

The core issue here is to redesign Kafka topics in a way that aligns with business domains rather than organizational structures. This involves understanding how business processes work and grouping related events into logical domains. The key aspects the interviewer might be probing are:

Business Domain Alignment: Ensuring each domain has its own set of events.
Topic Ownership: Defining clear ownership for topics to avoid chatty cross-team communication.
Team Topologies: Redesigning team structures to better support these domains.

#### Assumptions
The current organizational structure is well-defined and can be mapped to business domains.
Each business domain has specific processes and events that need to be captured, transformed, or consumed.
Teams are responsible for certain business domains and have domain experts who understand the workflow.

#### First-Principles Solution
Step 1: Identify Business Domains

First, we need to identify and define clear business domains. For example:

Sales: Customer interactions, sales leads, order processing.
Marketing: Campaign management, customer engagement, analytics.
Customer Support: Ticket creation, resolution, follow-ups.
Step 2: Map Events to Business Domains

Next, we map the events generated by each process within a domain. For instance:

Sales could have topics like salesLead, orderPlaced, and customerFeedback.
Marketing might have campaignStart, emailSent, and leadGenerated.
Step 3: Design Topics Based on Business Domains

Design the Kafka topics based on these business domains. This reduces cross-team chatter by ensuring that each team only consumes and produces events relevant to their domain:

Step 4: Define Topic Ownership

For each topic, define clear ownership. This can be done by:

Assigning Teams: Each team should own the topics related to their domain.
Role-Based Access Control (RBAC): Ensure only authorized personnel have access to produce or consume events on these topics.
Step 5: Implement Logical Filters and Gateways

Implement filters and gateways at the boundaries of each business domain. These can be used for:

Filtering out irrelevant events from other domains.
Ensuring data consistency across different systems.

Step 6: Monitor and Optimize

Continuously monitor the system to ensure that topics are being used effectively. Optimize topic structures as needed based on feedback from teams:

Latency: Ensure topics are not causing latency issues.
Volume: Check if the volume of events is manageable.

#### Closing Summary

By reshaping team topologies and topic ownership, we can align Kafka topics with business domains rather than organizational hierarchies. This approach ensures more efficient communication and collaboration within teams and across them, making the system more robust and maintainable.

#### Common Mistakes/Follow-Ups
Failing to Clearly Define Domains: Without a clear understanding of business processes, domain boundaries can become blurry.
Overlapping Topics: Defining too many overlapping topics can lead to chatty communication and increased complexity.
Ignoring RBAC: Failing to properly manage access control can result in security breaches or data misuse.

If the interviewer probes further, you might discuss implementing more sophisticated patterns like Event Sourcing, Command Query Responsibility Segregation (CQRS), or even integrating with other domain-driven design principles.
