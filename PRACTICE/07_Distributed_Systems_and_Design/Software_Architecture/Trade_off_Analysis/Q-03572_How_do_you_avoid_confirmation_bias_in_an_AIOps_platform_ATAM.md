# How do you avoid confirmation bias in an AIOps platform ATAM when the evaluation team recently built a similar observability-and-detection stack elsewhere and unconsciously validates the same TSDB, sampling, and correlation choices? What structural safeguards do you build into the review?

**Difficulty:** Deep · **Levels:** Architect/Principal, SDE-2/3 · [↑ Facilitating an ATAM](<../../../../07_Distributed_Systems_and_Design/Software_Architecture/Trade_off_Analysis/Facilitating_an_ATAM.md>)

## Answer

When performing an Architectural Trade-off Analysis (ATAM) in AIOps, especially when evaluating a new platform against a recently built observability-and-detection stack, it's crucial to avoid confirmation bias. This involves ensuring that the evaluation process is fair, objective, and unbiased. To achieve this, we need to build several structural safeguards into the review process.

Firstly, let’s break down what ATAM entails in an AIOps context. ATAM helps us identify and evaluate trade-offs between different architectural choices such as time-series database (TSDB) selection, sampling strategies, correlation algorithms, and more. The goal is to make informed decisions that best meet the operational needs of the platform.

To avoid confirmation bias, we need to ensure that the evaluation team:

Has a diverse background: Team members should come from different technical backgrounds and have expertise in various aspects of AIOps.
Remains objective: Team members must be open to questioning their own assumptions and be willing to consider alternative viewpoints.
Uses structured methodologies: Employing standardized evaluation criteria can help keep the review process fair.

Now, let’s look at some specific structural safeguards we can build into the review:

1. Establish a Clear Evaluation Framework

Create a detailed framework that outlines all possible trade-offs and their potential impacts on various metrics such as performance, scalability, reliability, and maintainability. This framework should be agreed upon by all team members before the evaluation begins.

2. Implement Blind Reviews (If Possible)

For critical decisions, consider having blind reviews where certain parts of the platform are reviewed without revealing the source or the team’s initial biases. This can help uncover any unconscious validation.

3. Include Peer Review and External Validation

Engage peers from other departments or external experts to provide an independent perspective. Their feedback can challenge assumptions and highlight potential biases that might have been overlooked.

4. Use Simulation and Testing

Simulate various scenarios using the same TSDB, sampling, and correlation choices used in the existing stack. Compare these simulations with those generated by the new platform. This helps ensure a balanced comparison without favoring one over the other.

5. Document and Share Assumptions Explicitly

Ensure that all assumptions made during the evaluation process are documented clearly. This transparency can help others understand the reasoning behind decisions and identify any potential biases.

6. Regular Feedback Loops

Conduct regular feedback sessions with the team to discuss emerging insights and adjust the evaluation framework if necessary. This iterative approach helps refine the review process over time.

#### Closing Summary

By implementing these structural safeguards, we can significantly reduce confirmation bias in our ATAM for AIOps platforms. The key is to maintain a high level of transparency, objectivity, and continuous improvement in the evaluation process.

#### Common Mistakes/Follow-Ups

Failing to Document Assumptions: Not documenting assumptions can lead to unchecked biases.
Lack of Independence: Relying too heavily on internal team members for evaluations can introduce subtle biases.
Overlooking Edge Cases: Focusing only on the most common use cases without considering edge scenarios might skew results.
